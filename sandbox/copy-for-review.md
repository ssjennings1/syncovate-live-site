# How I work with owners and their teams — advisory, facilitation, workshops, and retreats

When people ask why they should work with me, I always come back to one thing.

> "When someone really hears you — without judging you, without trying to fix you — it feels damn good. It is astonishing how elements which seem insoluble become soluble when someone listens."— Carl Rogers

It's where my why started. But here's what I learned along the way: being heard is only the first step. It opens the door. What you do next is what changes things. People need tools, and they need someone who can help take that clarity all the way to action.

That's the space we build together — insight, action, and momentum.

I've worked with founders, CEOs, and leadership teams for over 20 years — across trades, construction, manufacturing, family business, and professional services. The conversations are different every time. The pattern underneath them is remarkably consistent.

## Who finds their way here.

**The Founder / CEO**

### You built something real. Now you're not sure you're running it right.

You've gotten this far on instinct and sheer will, but somewhere along the way the way you've always done it stopped working — and you're carrying that alone. I've had this conversation with owners running $2M service companies and $40M manufacturers. The words are different. The weight is the same.

"Am I doing this right? Am I going to hurt people? Am I going to screw it up?"

**The Middle Manager**

### Great at the work, still figuring out the leading.

You got promoted because you're good — really good. But being good at the job and being good at leading the people who do the job are completely different skills. You need someone who can help you build your toolbox and find your voice.

"I know my stuff. I just don't always know how to get people to follow me."

**The Rising Gen**

### You're stepping into something your family built, and that's a lot.

Caught between what was built before you and what you want to build next, you need someone who gets all of it — not just the business part.

"I want to honor what was built and still make it mine."

## It doesn't matter what we call it.

Coaching, advising, consulting, facilitation — those words get watered down. What matters is what happens when we work together. Here's what that looks like — whether it's a room of twelve or just you.

### You feel heard. Really heard.

Not managed. Not judged. I'm genuinely curious about what you're seeing, what you're carrying, and what you almost named but couldn't quite get to. I ask questions until the picture gets clear.

### You see something you couldn't see before.

Not because I told you what it was. Because together we decoded what was already there — connecting dots from things you've said, things your people have said, things that happened years ago that are still showing up today. And then you see it. And it's obvious.

### You leave with something every time — an insight, an action, a strategy.

Insight without action is just a nice conversation. We don't stop there. Once the smoke clears, we figure out the next real move together. No 40-page deck. No jargon. Just what's next.

### You did it. Not me.

That's the part I love most and get the least credit for. The magic is yours. I just create the conditions for it to happen. You had this in you the whole time — you just needed the space to think and a partner.

# Dr. Shannon Jennings · Dr. J

I love the way entrepreneurs think.

I've spent my whole career around people who built something from nothing — and I never get tired of it. The way their brains work, connecting a supplier problem to something their kid said to a podcast they half-heard — that energizes me. It's what gets me out of bed Monday morning, excited to see what the week brings.

**Let's Talk**

## The problem people bring you is almost never the real problem.

I learned that early, working with families in the hardest situations — divorce, custody, the stuff where somebody's kids are on the line. The real problem was the structure around them. The unspoken rules. What everybody knew behind closed doors but nobody said in the hallway.

I carried that into business at the Edward Lowe Foundation, running peer retreats for entrepreneurs and CEOs. Their whole mission was to champion the entrepreneurial spirit — and I'm still living that mission more than a decade after I left. Because these companies matter. The ones growing in your town, hiring locally, sponsoring the little leagues, putting money back in — they hold communities together, especially now, when a lot of other things are falling apart. That's who I've built my work around.

Here's what I've learned in all those rooms: when you build something, you're in it. The business and the person aren't two separate things. Your money, your identity, your relationships, how you sleep — it's all tied up in the thing you made. That's true for the trades contractor and the manufacturer and the professional-services owner, and it gets even more tangled in a family business, where the succession talk is also a family talk and the org chart and the dinner table are the same table. You have to see the whole picture to be any real use in any of it. That's the work I love most.

## Why a Business Psychologist?

My doctorate in business psychology put real, evidence-based structure around what twenty years of closed doors had already taught me.

I kept seeing the same thing: leaders with the best of intentions, working incredibly hard, and their teams were fracturing and the work was stalling out. The assumption was always a people problem — fix the difficult personality, hire better talent.

Look closer and it's rarely a people problem. It's an architecture problem.

When you build a company from scratch, the system organizes itself around you. The processes, the communication patterns, the way authority flows — all of it made sense when the company was small. Then the business grows, and the structure that helped it survive starts to suffocate it. You built it, which makes you the worst person to see what's broken in it. Not because anything's wrong with you. Because you're inside it.

You can't fix what you can't see from inside. My job is to be the person who can see the architecture of your blind spots — and stay in the room until something actually moves.

## Direct. Not one-sided. In it with you.

If you want someone to nod along and tell you the plan looks great, I'm not the right fit.

**I listen first.**

Long enough that you'll probably say the thing you weren't planning to say. Then I say my piece, plainly.

**I don't take sides.**

Not the owner's, not the team's, not the family's. That's usually why people open up in the first place.

**I won't talk down to you.**

If you built your leadership by doing it instead of studying it, you know more than you're giving yourself credit for. I'm not here to replace what you've got. I'm here to help you see it clearly and use it better.

**I stay.**

Insight is the easy part. The hard part is next Tuesday, when you have to do something with it. I'm in the room for that, too.

The work takes whatever shape the situation calls for. One session to get a stuck decision unstuck. A year of monthly calls that become the one place you can think out loud without consequences. Me in a room with your team when they've stopped being honest with each other. What it isn't: therapy — that's a different practice — or a program you work through on a schedule. And when I stop being useful, I'll say so.

The Neuroscience of High-Performing Teams

When technology and AI eliminate operational friction, the remaining friction is entirely human. Many organizations mistake speed for effectiveness and polite agreement for alignment. In this session, Dr. J examines why healthy teams actually require friction, how leaders accidentally create "polite avoidance," and how to cultivate the psychological safety that allows hard truths to be spoken out loud without blowing up relationships.

Best for: Executive retreats, cross-functional leadership gatherings, industry associations navigating rapid technological change.

### The Architecture of the Blind Spot

Why Smart Companies Build Systems That Work Against Them

In twenty years of organizational work, the biggest breakdowns rarely stem from bad intentions. They stem from good intentions that created quiet, unintended structures underneath them. As companies grow through the $2M to $40M corridor, the communication loops, decision pathways, and authority structures that once enabled survival begin to suffocate scalability. Dr. J breaks down how to see what your daily proximity hides from you.

Best for: Entrepreneurial networks, privately held company summits, family enterprise conferences.

Key takeaway: A diagnostic framework to identify whether a recurring breakdown is an individual performance issue or a structural architecture problem.
